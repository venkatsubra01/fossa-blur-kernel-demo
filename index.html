Blurring in four ideas

1Pictures are gridsA digital photo is a grid of tiny coloured squares called pixels, each with a red, green and blue amount.
2Blur = averaging neighboursTo blur, we rebuild the picture one pixel at a time: each new pixel is a mix of the old pixels around it.
3The kernel is the recipeThe kernel says how much each neighbour counts (its share of the vote). Shares always add up to 100%.
4p sets the recipe's shapeGaussian: close neighbours count more, a soft hill. Disk: everyone inside a circle counts equally, like an out-of-focus lens.

What the paper does

FOSSA learns depth from focus stacks: photos of the same scene focused at different distances. There aren't many real focus stacks to train on, so the authors make synthetic ones by blurring regular photos, with stronger blur for objects farther from the focus plane (the blur radius c is the circle of confusion).

Earlier work blurred with a Gaussian only. Real lenses behave differently: when blur is small, diffraction makes it look Gaussian, and when blur is large, geometric optics makes it look like a uniform disk. The paper combines both in one formula (Eq. 7):

F(u, v, p) = 1⁄c² · exp( −2 · ( (u² + v²) / c² )p/2 )

Here (u, v) is a neighbour's offset from the centre. With p = 2 you get the Gaussian; as p → ∞ the term is 0 inside the circle and huge outside, so you get the disk. During training, FOSSA draws p ~ 2U(1,5) (between 2 and 32) so the model works on many different lenses. As in FOSSA's renderer, the shares are normalized to sum to 1, and near the image border, neighbours that fall outside the image are skipped and the rest are re-normalized.
